The problem

A raw key has no cap and no off-switch.

Companies now run fleets of autonomous agents that pay for things — APIs, compute, vendors. The moment one holds a key, the money is exposed.

01

No ceiling

A leaked key drains everything it can reach. There is no per-payment cap and no daily budget standing between the agent and your balance.

02

No off-switch

Cutting a compromised agent today means touching every service by hand — rotating keys, revoking scopes, hoping you caught them all.

03

No record

When an agent spends, there's rarely an independent, tamper-evident log of what it paid, to whom, and whether the payment was allowed.

The honest model

Control and independence — not trustlessness.

LEASH is a corporate-card model for agents. We're precise about what the chain does and what the facilitator does.

How enforcement actually works read the fine print ▾
→
The chain stores the policy and the revocation. Your limits live in the agent's ENS record; a revoke is one on-chain write. The facilitator LEASH runs enforces the limits you set on each payment.
→
Identity is on-chain-resolved, not verified. We resolve the agent's ERC-8004 / EVM identity from the chain — we don't attest that the operator behind it is who they claim.
→
Rolling caps are a soft budget. Daily and weekly ceilings are best-effort accounting, not an exact trustless guarantee. The per-payment cap is the hard limit.
→
Privy denies over-funding on an independent rail. Funding is a separate path; an over-fund is blocked before it goes out. Privy does not co-sign payments.
→
Co-owned, 2-of-2. The agent holds its own key; LEASH holds only its co-signer key plus the agent's public key. Neither can spend alone.
→
LEASH binds agents, it doesn't mint them. You bring an agent you already run; we bind it to identity, an account, and limits.
