The problem

A raw key has no cap and no off-switch.

Companies now run fleets of agents that pay for things on their own. The moment you hand an agent a key, you've handed it your wallet — with no ceiling and no brakes.

  • ✕An agent with a raw key can spend without limit, at any hour, to anyone.
  • ✕A leaked key keeps paying until you notice — and revoking it means touching every service by hand.
  • ✕No single place shows what the fleet spent, what got blocked, or who was cut off.
How it works

Four plain beats.

Bind

Point LEASH at an agent you already run. It binds the agent's on-chain-resolved identity and a co-owned account.

Declare

Set the limits: a hard per-payment cap, optional soft daily/weekly budgets, and active hours.

Enforce

The facilitator LEASH runs checks every payment against the policy the chain stores. Over-limit fails closed.

Revoke

One on-chain write cuts the agent off everywhere. The next payment is blocked. Reversible.

The one-write kill

Same agent, same payment. Before the write it pays. After a single on-chain revoke, the policy record is gone and the next identical payment fails closed.

The honest model

What's true — and what isn't.

How LEASH actually enforces (read before you trust it) ›
  • →This is a corporate-card model, not trustless. The chain stores the policy and records the revocation; the facilitator LEASH runs enforces the limits you set.
  • →Identity is on-chain-resolved, not "verified" — LEASH resolves the agent's identity from the chain.
  • →Rolling daily/weekly caps are a soft budget. The hard per-payment cap is the firm ceiling.
  • →Privy is the independent funding rail and denies an over-fund before it goes out. Privy does not co-sign payments.
  • →The account is co-owned (2-of-2): the agent holds its own key; LEASH holds its co-signer key plus the agent's public key. Neither can spend alone.
  • →LEASH binds agents you already run — it doesn't mint them.
